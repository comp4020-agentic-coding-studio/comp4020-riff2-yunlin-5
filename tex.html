Started 1978, still running

What is TeX, and why does it still matter?

TeX is a typesetting program: you write plain-text markup describing a document, and TeX turns it into finished pages with correct hyphenation, even spacing, and mathematics set the way a human typesetter would set it. Most people today reach it through LaTeX, a set of macros built on top that makes the plain TeX language easier to write in.

Why it exists

In the late 1970s Donald Knuth, a computer scientist rather than a typesetter, was revising the second volume of The Art of Computer Programming. His publisher's new digital typesetting system came back with proofs so much worse than the first edition that he balked at them. Around the same time he saw a book that had been typeset well by computer and made the connection: typesetting is just arranging ink and no-ink correctly. He thought a proper solution would take about six months.

It took closer to ten years, with help along the way from typeface designers including Hermann Zapf, Chuck Bigelow, Kris Holmes, and Matthew Carter. The result was two programs: TeX for laying out text and mathematics, and Metafont for describing the fonts themselves. Both were released for no fee, with source available to anyone, at a time when the commercial alternatives were expensive, proprietary, and often machine-specific.

Why it lasted

The feature that mattered most in the long run was not the typography. It was the promise underneath it: the same input file produces the same output, the same line breaks and the same page breaks, on any machine, forever. That is unusual. It is why a thesis written in LaTeX in 1990 will still typeset correctly today, and why the format never became beholden to any one company's software surviving.

TeX itself has been frozen for years, deliberately: past a certain point, stability outranked new features. The active development happens around it, in LaTeX, in the fonts, and in the free TeX Live distribution TUG helps coordinate.

Where TUG comes in

TeX is free and always has been, but somebody still has to package it, test it on three operating systems, run the archive it downloads from, publish the documentation, and pay for the meetings where the people who maintain it talk to each other. That is the work TUG funds, out of membership dues rather than sales, since 1980.
